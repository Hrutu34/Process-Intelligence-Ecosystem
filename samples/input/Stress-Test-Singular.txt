A large manufacturing company has a centralized procurement process for purchasing materials, software, services, spare parts, and other items required by its plants and corporate departments. The process usually starts when an employee realizes that something is needed, although in some cases the requirement is generated automatically by the inventory management system when stock falls below a predefined reorder point. There are therefore two slightly different ways the process can begin, but both eventually result in a purchase requisition being created in the ERP system.

For normal employee-initiated requests, the employee opens the procurement portal, selects the approximate category of the item, describes what is required, specifies the required-by date, quantity, estimated cost, cost center, and sometimes a preferred supplier. If the request is for a recurring item, the employee may simply copy a previous requisition. The system performs some basic validation at this stage. It checks whether the requester is an active employee, whether the cost center exists, whether the required-by date is valid, and whether the requested quantity is greater than zero. If any of those validations fail, the requester is shown an error and must correct the request before it can be submitted.

For inventory-triggered requests, the inventory management system sends a replenishment message to the ERP system. The ERP creates a draft requisition containing the material number, plant, required quantity, and suggested delivery date. A warehouse planner reviews these automatically generated requisitions. If the planner agrees with the requirement, they release the requisition for approval. If the planner believes the system generated the request incorrectly, perhaps because stock is already on order or because the demand forecast changed, they can reject or cancel it. Cancelled replenishment requests do not continue through procurement.

Once a requisition has been submitted or released, the ERP determines the approval route based primarily on estimated value, category, and organizational ownership. Requests below INR 25,000 can normally be approved by the employee's line manager. Requests from INR 25,000 up to INR 250,000 require both the line manager and the relevant budget owner to approve. Requests above INR 250,000 additionally require procurement approval, and requests above INR 1,000,000 require a senior procurement manager as well as the finance controller. These thresholds are configurable and can change over time, so the system uses the current approval matrix rather than hard-coding the rules into the request itself.

There are also category-specific rules. IT hardware and software purchases are routed to the IT asset team for review even if their value is low. Purchases involving personal data, security services, or certain regulated materials are sent to the appropriate compliance or information-security reviewer. A purchase can therefore have more than one approval requirement at the same time.

The first approver receives an email and an application notification. The approver can approve, reject, or send the requisition back for clarification. If it is sent back, the requester receives a notification explaining what information is missing. The requester edits the requisition and resubmits it, after which the approval process starts again. If the approver rejects the request outright, the requisition is closed as rejected and the requester is notified. Rejected requisitions can technically be copied into a new requisition later, but that is considered a new process instance rather than a continuation of the old one.

Where multiple approvals are required, some approvals can happen in parallel. For example, the budget owner and IT asset team may review the request at the same time. However, procurement approval cannot begin until all mandatory upstream approvals have been completed. If any parallel reviewer rejects the request, the overall requisition is normally rejected, although the requester may be allowed to correct the request if the rejection was caused by missing information rather than an actual business prohibition.

The system periodically checks approval deadlines. A standard approver has two business days to act. After one business day, a reminder is sent. After two business days, the request is escalated to the approver's manager. If there is still no action after another business day, procurement operations is notified and may manually reassign the approval. During public holidays, the deadline calculation uses the corporate business calendar rather than elapsed calendar time. If an approver is marked as being on leave, the system may automatically route the approval to a configured delegate.

Once all required approvals are complete, procurement operations determines whether a competitive sourcing exercise is required. This depends on the category, supplier status, estimated value, and company sourcing policy. For example, a low-value purchase from an already approved supplier may proceed without competitive bidding. A high-value purchase generally requires quotations from multiple suppliers. Some strategic categories require a formal request-for-proposal process regardless of amount.

If competitive sourcing is not required, the process proceeds to purchase-order preparation.

If sourcing is required, a buyer creates a sourcing event. The buyer identifies potential suppliers and sends them a request for quotation or proposal. Suppliers submit quotations through the supplier portal or, in exceptional cases, by email. The buyer records the quotations in the sourcing system. If fewer than the minimum required number of valid quotations are received by the deadline, the buyer can extend the deadline, contact additional suppliers, or request a sourcing-policy exception. The sourcing-policy exception must itself be approved by the procurement manager. If the exception is denied and insufficient quotations are still available, the requisition is returned to the requester or cancelled.

When sufficient quotations have been collected, the buyer evaluates them. Price is important but is not necessarily the only criterion; delivery time, technical compliance, warranty, supplier risk, and payment terms may also be considered. For some categories, technical evaluation is performed by an engineering or business stakeholder before commercial evaluation is finalized. The buyer prepares a recommendation identifying the preferred supplier and the reasons for selection.

If the selected supplier is new to the company, supplier onboarding must take place before a purchase order can be issued. The supplier provides legal information, tax details, bank information, certifications, and other required documents. Procurement verifies the information, while finance performs bank-account and tax-related checks. Depending on the supplier's category, compliance or risk teams may also perform due diligence. If the supplier fails onboarding, the buyer may select the next-best supplier from the sourcing exercise, provided the sourcing rules allow this. Otherwise, a new sourcing exercise may be required.

For an existing supplier, procurement checks whether an active contract or framework agreement exists. If there is an applicable contract, the purchase should normally reference it. If there is no contract and the category requires one, the legal team becomes involved. Legal reviews the proposed terms, negotiates changes with the supplier where necessary, and eventually marks the contract as approved. Negotiations can involve several back-and-forth iterations, and the process may return repeatedly to legal review until both sides agree. If legal determines that the purchase cannot proceed, the requisition is cancelled.

After supplier selection and any necessary contract work, procurement creates the purchase order in the ERP system. The purchase order contains supplier details, line items, quantities, prices, delivery location, requested delivery date, payment terms, tax information, and references to the underlying requisition and contract if applicable.

Before the purchase order is released, the ERP performs a final set of automated checks. It verifies that the requisition is fully approved, the supplier is active, the purchase order value does not exceed the approved amount beyond an allowed tolerance, mandatory contract references are present where required, and the accounting information is valid. If the purchase order fails any automated check, it is sent back to procurement for correction. Some errors can be fixed by the buyer directly; others require the requisition to be reopened and potentially re-approved.

Once the purchase order passes validation, it is released and sent to the supplier electronically. The supplier is expected to acknowledge the order. The supplier may accept the order, reject it, or accept it with changes. If the supplier accepts it without changes, the order proceeds normally. If the supplier proposes changes to quantity, price, delivery date, or other material commercial terms, procurement reviews the proposed changes. Minor changes within predefined tolerance may be accepted by the buyer, but material changes can require the budget owner or original approvers to approve the revised commitment. If the revised price exceeds the approved tolerance, the purchase order may have to be cancelled and recreated or the underlying requisition may have to go through approval again.

If the supplier does not acknowledge the purchase order within three business days, the procurement system sends a reminder. After five business days, the buyer is notified. The buyer can contact the supplier manually, resend the order, or escalate the issue to the supplier relationship manager. The process does not automatically cancel the order merely because an acknowledgement is missing.

After the supplier accepts the order, the supplier delivers the goods or performs the service. For physical goods, the receiving warehouse records a goods receipt in the ERP. For services, the responsible employee or service owner records a service confirmation. Partial deliveries are allowed, so multiple receipts can be recorded against the same purchase order. If the delivered quantity is lower than the ordered quantity, the purchase order remains open for the outstanding quantity unless the buyer explicitly closes the remaining balance.

At receipt, the warehouse checks quantity and, where applicable, packaging and visible condition. If the goods are damaged or materially different from what was ordered, the warehouse places them into a blocked or quarantine status and informs procurement. Procurement contacts the supplier and decides whether the goods should be returned, replaced, accepted with a commercial adjustment, or inspected further. A replacement shipment creates another delivery cycle. If the goods are accepted after inspection, the receipt can be posted normally.

For certain materials, quality inspection is mandatory. In that case, the quality team receives a notification after physical receipt. Quality either accepts the material, accepts it with restrictions, or rejects it. Rejected material cannot be consumed until disposition is decided. Depending on the reason, procurement may request replacement from the supplier, return the material, or obtain an approved deviation from the engineering or quality authority. A rejected quality result can therefore create a loop back to supplier interaction without necessarily closing the purchase order.

After a valid receipt or service confirmation exists, the supplier submits an invoice. Invoices may arrive electronically through an invoice portal, through an integrated supplier network, or occasionally as email/PDF documents that are processed by accounts payable.

Accounts payable first checks whether the invoice can be associated with a valid supplier and purchase order. It then performs invoice validation. For standard purchases, the company uses a three-way match between the purchase order, goods receipt or service confirmation, and supplier invoice. The system compares quantities, prices, taxes, and other relevant fields.

If the three-way match succeeds within configured tolerances, the invoice is automatically approved for payment. If the invoice fails because of a price or quantity discrepancy, the invoice is placed on hold and an exception workflow starts. Accounts payable determines whether the problem is caused by an incorrect invoice, a missing receipt, an incorrect purchase order, or an actual commercial dispute.

If a receipt is missing even though the goods have physically arrived, the receiving team is asked to post the receipt. If the purchase order contains an incorrect price, procurement may need to correct the purchase order, provided the correction is permitted under company policy. If the supplier invoice is wrong, the supplier is asked to issue a corrected invoice or credit note. If the difference represents a genuine dispute, procurement and the business owner investigate it and agree on a resolution. Some disputes require supplier negotiation and can take several cycles.

If an invoice exceeds the purchase order by more than the allowed tolerance, additional approval may be required even if the underlying purchase was otherwise valid. The finance controller can approve certain exceptions. Other exceptions require procurement approval or budget-owner approval depending on the reason and value.

There is also a special path for invoices received before a goods receipt exists. In those cases, accounts payable may hold the invoice. If the goods are later received within a reasonable period, the invoice can automatically re-enter matching. If the goods never arrive and the purchase order is eventually cancelled, the invoice is rejected or returned to the supplier.

Once an invoice is approved, it enters the payment queue. The payment date depends on the supplier's contractual payment terms, applicable early-payment discounts, cash-management rules, and scheduled payment runs. Finance generates a payment proposal, performs payment controls, and releases the payment. Certain high-value payments require additional authorization before the bank file can be transmitted.

If payment execution fails, for example because of invalid bank details or a banking-system error, the payment is marked as failed. Finance investigates the reason, and supplier master data may need to be corrected. If bank details are changed, the change itself is subject to a separate verification and approval process. After the issue is resolved, the invoice is returned to a future payment run rather than being treated as a new invoice.

After successful payment, the ERP records the payment against the invoice and closes the invoice. The purchase order is closed when all expected receipts and invoices are complete, or when procurement explicitly closes any remaining balance. If there is a remaining open quantity that will never be delivered, procurement may close the balance after obtaining the appropriate business approval.

At the end of the process, procurement and finance retain the requisition, approval history, sourcing documents, purchase order, contract references, receipts, quality records, invoice, and payment information according to the company's retention policy. Some records are retained for audit purposes even when the transaction itself is cancelled.

The process therefore has several possible end states: the requisition can be rejected before ordering; a sourcing exercise can fail and the request can be cancelled; a supplier can fail onboarding; legal can prevent a purchase from proceeding; a purchase order can be cancelled after supplier negotiation; goods can be returned while the order remains open; an invoice can be rejected; or, in the normal successful case, the supplier is paid and the purchase order is eventually closed.

There are also a few unusual cases that should not be overlooked. An employee can withdraw a requisition before final approval. Procurement can cancel an approved requisition before a purchase order is issued if the business no longer needs the item. A purchase order can be cancelled after issuance if the supplier agrees, but cancellation may require approval depending on the value. In emergencies, an authorized emergency-procurement route can bypass some normal sourcing steps, although it cannot bypass mandatory financial controls and must be retrospectively reviewed. The emergency route is therefore a variant of the normal process rather than an entirely separate process.

Finally, the company periodically reviews procurement performance. This is not part of every individual transaction, but procurement analytics aggregate data from completed and cancelled transactions to measure approval time, sourcing cycle time, supplier delivery performance, invoice exception rates, payment performance, and overall purchase-to-pay compliance. These metrics are reviewed monthly by procurement leadership and finance, and significant problems may result in changes to approval rules, supplier policies, sourcing thresholds, or process configuration.
